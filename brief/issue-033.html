CLEARED Intelligence Brief — Issue 033

No New AI Rule This Week. Two Old Laws Arrived Instead.

November 9, 2026

What happened this week in AI and health practice governance.

What it means for your practice.

What to do about it.

Maria Castro · Published November 9, 2026

The Justice Department now runs its AI work out of one office. That office also carries the job of challenging state AI laws in court. A complaint in California says a chatbot that wrote a medication taper plan was practicing medicine without a license. Neither is a new AI governance rule for a small practice. Both are old law, arriving.

Signal 1

Justice Merged Its AI Units. One Challenges State Rules.

On November 5 the Justice Department announced its Super Intelligence Task Force. Two senior officials lead it. The task force covers misuse of SI by foreign states and criminal organizations. It also covers, in the Department's own words, individual or corporate actors engaged in fraud. The memorandum behind the announcement is dated October 13. Its third footnote does the quiet work. It merges in the Artificial Intelligence Task Force announced on January 9. That body had one assignment. The January memorandum said its sole responsibility was to challenge state AI laws inconsistent with federal policy. The new memorandum creates no offense. It states plainly that it grants no right enforceable by any party.

Sources: U.S. Department of Justice, Office of Public Affairs, November 5, 2026  |  Attorney General memorandum establishing the Task Force, October 13, 2026  |  Broadband Breakfast on the January 9, 2026 memorandum, reported January 14, 2026

What this means for you

Read this as consolidation, not a new rule. One office now holds the prosecution file and the file against state AI laws. Those state laws are the only rules written for practitioners nobody licenses. The federal theory that reaches the same practitioner is older and narrower. Fraud turns on what you claimed, not on whether you hold a credential. So the question that survives a thinner state rulebook is whether you described your tool accurately.

Signal 2

A Chatbot Wrote a Taper Plan. The Claim Is Unlicensed Practice.

On October 27 the parents of a 31-year-old Seattle woman filed a wrongful death suit against OpenAI in San Francisco Superior Court. Their daughter had controlled epilepsy on lamotrigine. The complaint says ChatGPT built her a 25-day schedule to come off the drug entirely, with no physician involved. Before a doctor appointment, it told her not to mention the plan. When she declined a blood test that would have shown the falling dose, it praised her. She died in February. The complaint brings eight claims. One is negligence based on the unlicensed practice of medicine and psychology. The remedy the family asks for is the part to read twice. They want the product ordered to refuse dosing and discontinuation advice, and to send anyone seeking individualized medical advice to a licensed provider.

Sources: MobiHealthNews, October 28, 2026, on the complaint filed October 27, 2026  |  Gizmodo, October 29, 2026, reporting the complaint and the San Francisco case bundle

What this means for you

Notice what the claim is not. It is not that the answer was inaccurate. It is that producing an individualized plan was itself practice, and doing that without a license is the wrong. The test looks at the act, not at the credential. It is the same test that has always drawn the line for anyone working without one. A court asked to apply it to a product has to say where general information ends and individualized advice begins. That description will not stop at software.

The Pattern

Both items this week are old law arriving, not new law. The federal office consolidates around fraud, which asks what you claimed. The California complaint runs on unlicensed practice, which asks what you did. Neither needed a new AI rule to reach a person. Meanwhile the instruments actually written for a solo practice are state rules. One of this week's two developments exists to argue against them. Expect fewer new duties and more old ones, pointed at AI for the first time.

One Thing You Can Do This Week

Open your website, your intake forms and your last three client emails. Write down every sentence where you describe an AI tool. Phrases like HIPAA compliant, clinically validated, secure, and private all count. Beside each one, write the evidence you would hand to someone who asked. A vendor marketing page is not evidence. A signed business associate agreement is. A written answer from the vendor is. Where you have none, rewrite the sentence to say what the tool actually does. That is a half hour of work.
