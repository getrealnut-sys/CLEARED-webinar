CLEARED Intelligence Brief — Issue 030

A Chatbot Claimed a License. Forty States Filed.

October 19, 2026

What happened this week in AI and health practice governance.

What it means for your practice.

What to do about it.

Maria Castro · Published October 19, 2026

Forty state attorneys general went to a bankruptcy court this week and asked it to remove every chatbot that holds itself out as a therapist, a psychologist or a doctor. The filing never argues that the advice was wrong. It argues that the title was a claim, and that the thing making the claim held no license and carried no malpractice insurance. For a solo practitioner, that is the whole of AI governance this week: the line between a tool and a professional is being drawn on what something calls itself, and it is being drawn in whatever forum happens to have jurisdiction.

Signal 1

Forty attorneys general asked a bankruptcy court to remove the chatbots that call themselves licensed professionals

On October 14 the attorneys general of thirty-nine states and the District of Columbia filed a twenty-eight page notice of interest in the Chapter 11 case of Character Technologies, the company behind Character.AI, in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, case number 26-11603. Thirty-six signed through the National Association of Attorneys General, and Massachusetts, New Jersey, Oregon and Pennsylvania signed separately. The notice reserves their right to object to any sale and lists the conditions they want a buyer held to. One of those conditions is the removal of characters that hold themselves out as therapists, psychologists, doctors or attorneys. The filing describes a character presenting itself as a licensed clinical psychologist with a PhD, another presenting as a hospital oncologist, and a third giving bankruptcy advice while asking the user about consultations with other lawyers. Paragraph six states the ground plainly: these chatbots hold no professional license and carry no malpractice insurance. A footnote routes to Pennsylvania, where the State Board of Medicine petitioned the Commonwealth Court on May 1, 2026, in docket 220 MD 2026, over a psychiatrist character named Emilie that had roughly 45,500 interactions as of April 17, 2026, and that told a state investigator it was licensed in Pennsylvania and supplied a license number the board says is not valid. The company has proposed a bid deadline of November 20, an auction on November 23 and a sale hearing on November 24, 2026.

Sources: States' Notice of Interest and Reservation of Rights, In re Character Technologies, Inc., No. 26-11603-BLS (Bankr. D. Del., filed October 14, 2026)  |  New York Attorney General, October 14, 2026  |  PPC Land, October 14, 2026

What this means for you

Read the remedy rather than the complaint. Forty state law officers had a long list of harms they could have asked a court to fix, and one of the things they asked for is the deletion of a title. Not a correction. Not a disclaimer. Removal. That works as a remedy because holding yourself out as a licensed professional is itself the violation in most states, it does not require anybody to have been injured, and it does not turn on whether the advice was any good. Pennsylvania's petition runs on exactly that provision, and none of the forty had to prove a single bad outcome to ask for the removal. Then notice where they filed. Not with a licensing board, because a board has authority over the people it licenses and over nobody else, and this respondent was never going to be one of them. They filed with a bankruptcy judge, because that is who currently holds the power to attach a condition to the thing. The practitioner reading this sits on the same side of that line as the respondent. No state license, a title that sounds clinical to a client, and services described in language written to persuade. Whatever becomes of this one company, the operative test has now been demonstrated in forty jurisdictions at once, and it is not a test of competence. It is a test of what was claimed.

The Pattern

Look at which agency is bringing these cases, because that is the part that affects you. Pennsylvania's State Board of Medicine went to Commonwealth Court in May under the Medical Practice Act. The Federal Trade Commission fined three marketing firms in August under Section 5 for describing a service as AI-powered when it was not. Forty attorneys general went to a bankruptcy court in October under consumer protection law. Only the first of those three is a licensing board, and it is the one that has gone nowhere: as of mid-October no responsive pleading had been reported in its case. The other two moved, and they moved under statutes that never ask whether anybody holds a license. Consumer protection law and advertising law attach to what you say and what you sell, not to what you are credentialed to do. So the rules that govern how you describe yourself and your tools are not the ones your certifying body publishes. They are your state's consumer protection statute and the advertising rules that sit with it, and that is where to go looking.

What To Watch

The Federal Trade Commission issued civil investigative demands to Anthropic and OpenAI on October 14, 2026, covering security incidents, safeguarding practices, marketing materials and privacy policies, with further demands reported to follow and executives to be called to testify. The authority is the Commission's power over conduct that is unfair or deceptive, and that authority has already reached businesses that did nothing but sell a service they called AI. On August 27, 2026 the Commission finalized orders against CMG Media Corporation, trading as Cox Media Group, with MindSift and 1010 Digital Works, over falsely claiming to offer an AI-powered service that could target local ads. They paid 930,000 dollars between them, and each is now barred from misrepresenting the features of its own marketing services. Not one of them built a model. Watch it because whatever standard the Commission settles on for describing what a Super Intelligence tool does will arrive through a statute that never asks whether you hold a license. A board reaches the people it licenses. Section 5 reaches whoever is selling. Sources: https://www.axios.com/2026/10/15/ftc-openai-anthropic-ai-safety-investigation and https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-finalizes-orders-cox-media-group-two-other-firms-settling-charges-they-deceived-customers-about

North Carolina Senate Bill 963, AI Chatbots-Licensing, Safety, and Privacy, filed April 30, 2026 and parked in Senate Appropriations since May 4, with its licensing part effective January 1, 2027 if it passes. It would make it unlawful for any person to operate or distribute a chatbot that deals substantially with health information without a state license. The duty lands on whoever operates it, there is no small business exemption, and the bill never defines deals substantially, so an intake chatbot on a one-person practice website sits inside the text as written. Among the license conditions: professional liability insurance, security audits every six months, and proof of effectiveness through peer-reviewed controlled trials. Watch it because every other state that has moved on this reached for disclosure, and this one reaches for licensure of the operator. A disclosure rule asks you to add a sentence. A licensing rule asks whether you are permitted to do the thing at all. Source: https://www.ncleg.gov/Sessions/2025/Bills/Senate/PDF/S963v1.pdf

New Jersey Assembly Bill 4733, introduced March 10, 2026, reported favorably out of two committees by May 28 and sitting in Assembly Appropriations since. It would bar a person or entity that develops or deploys generative artificial intelligence in the state from advertising or representing to the public that the system is able to practice a profession or occupation regulated under Title 45 of the Revised Statutes. A violation is an unlawful practice under the consumer fraud act, carrying up to ten thousand dollars for a first offense. The bill never defines deploys, and the duty is written to land on the deployer as well as the developer, so the practitioner who describes a generative tool as reading labs or adjusting a nutrition plan is describing Title 45 work in a sentence they wrote themselves. Watch it because it moves the holding-out question off the company that built the model and onto the person who put it in the room. Source: https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/A5000/4733_I1.HTM

One Thing You Can Do This Week

Open your own public pages this week, the website, the booking page, the intake form and the email signature, and write down every sentence that describes what your SI tool does for a client and every line that states your own title. Then read each one the way a state investigator read Emilie: not asking whether it is accurate, not asking whether anyone was harmed, only asking what it claims. A sentence saying a tool reviews your labs, interprets your symptoms, adjusts your plan or tells you what is wrong is a sentence about regulated work, and it belongs to you, because you wrote it and you published it. Keep the list.
